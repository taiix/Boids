# Reef Run's page must show, not promise

The funded multiplayer campaigns of 2025–2026 share one page shape: a gameplay-first video, a one-line pitch with a GIF, a Steam and Discord button pair near the top, the one mechanic that sets the game apart, short GIF-plus-two-sentence sections, rewards in the middle, and budget, roadmap and team last. Reef Run's plan fits that shape, and its €14,000 goal, 125/150/200/250% stretch steps and €12/€15 single-copy prices match the closest comparable almost exactly. Four things in the plan need to change: the four-pack is priced wrong (€12.25 per seat is no cheaper than the early bird), the early-bird limit of 300 is too high to ever close, the €29 and €199 tiers are weakly supported, and the campaign must run 30 days or less with Late Pledges off because Valve's key rules require it. The AI disclosure cannot be a "No AI" claim; the 2026 evidence says an up-front, code-only disclosure costs some backers but late discovery costs far more. The evidence does not support any measured rule about page layout, wishlist-badge placement, mobile share or first-48-hour thresholds, and it shows clearly that a well-built page with a demo still fails without an audience: a live campaign with nearly the same goal as Reef Run's sits at about 0%. Several Kickstarter rule pages could not be read by the researchers and must be checked in a browser before launch.

## Funded pages run pitch, buttons, hook, GIFs, rewards, then money and team

Thirteen funded and seven failed multiplayer campaign pages were read in order on 16 November 2026. The funded ones open with a welcome or "What is X?" block of one banner, one or two short sentences and a GIF. [The Boo Croo](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/synodic-arc/the-boo-croo-a-chaotic-4-player-party-game/description), a 4-player party game that raised $15,640 on a $10,000 goal, starts with a banner and the line "The Boo Croo is a spooktacular 4-player party game where teamwork and betrayal go hand-in-hand." [Springfield Hotel](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/msharkie/springfield-hotel-a-social-deduction-horror-game/description), which raised $84,908 on a $10,000 goal, puts its differentiator third on the page under "The Hotel is Always Listening", before any feature list. Reef Run's equivalent, that the shark hears the players' real voices on proximity chat, belongs in that same slot. The two failed social-deduction pages read ([Last One Lying](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/woidstudio/last-one-lying-social-deduction-party-game/description) and [Redhowl](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/benomstudio/redhowl/description)) showed no mechanic that differs from the format they copy.

The order below is the one most funded pages follow, adapted to Reef Run. It is a synthesis of observed pages, not a tested template.

| # | Block | What funded pages put there |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Project video | Gameplay within the first 10 seconds; real playtest voices with burned-in subtitles |
| 2 | Pitch | One line of real text plus one GIF that shows the premise without reading |
| 3 | Steam + Discord buttons | A pair of clickable images, once near the top |
| 4 | The hook mechanic | The shark hears your voice chat; one GIF, two sentences |
| 5 | How a round works | Fish side and shark side as separate GIF-plus-caption blocks, then how a round ends |
| 6 | Quick facts | Players, round length, platform, planned Steam price |
| 7 | Rewards | Tier-card graphic, per-seat price on packs |
| 8 | Stretch and community goals | All goals visible |
| 9 | What exists today / what €14,000 adds | Bullet list with numbers |
| 10 | Budget, roadmap | Pie by function; dated phases |
| 11 | Team | First person, photo, last third of the page |
| 12 | How it is made, including AI | Plain statement, repeated in the FAQ |
| 13 | Closing buttons | Steam + Discord pair again |

**Sections are short.** Most are a header banner, one image or GIF, and one to three sentences. Story text on funded small-goal pages runs from about 390 words ([Fat Goblins](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/kuju/fat-goblins-online-hat-based-pvp-inspired-by-fat-princess/description), a solo developer) to about 1,840 ([BLACKOUT: Zero Point](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/2138076565/blackout-zero-point-tactical-descent-into-chaos/description)), so the planned copy of roughly 1,500 words is in range. Banner-image headers are the norm; pages with plain text headings and few images look visibly less produced beside them. Team sections sit in the last third of every funded page read, and none opens with the team.

For an asymmetric game the closest working template is [Carnival Hunt](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/badraa/carnival-hunt/description) ($122,400 on a $50,000 goal): a hook sentence, a paragraph and GIF for the hunter, the same for the hunted, then the endgame. [Deadly Trick](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/finalblow/deadly-trick-a-reasoning-based-social-deduction-game/description) has the clearest round walkthrough, one sentence per image from round start to trial. No funded page used a numbered list for the loop. [Elevator](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/pixeltailgames/elevator-1/description) ($108,826 on $27,000) adds a five-line "Quick Facts" list with price point, playtime, platform and player count, and a "What We Have So Far" bullet list; both are cheap and worth copying.

**Copy should put the reader's own friend group in the scene.** The co-op hits of 2024–2026 spread through clips of friends panicking on proximity chat, and sit at $5–$20 on Steam because one purchase often becomes four ([AppMagic](https://appmagic.rocks/blog/friendslop-steam-games-2025)). That evidence comes from Steam launches, not crowdfunding, so applying it to a Kickstarter page is inference. GIFs are silent, so captions should quote what was said, and the video should carry the real voices. State the planned Steam price so the €15 tier reads as fair. Older developer postmortems agree on one elevator pitch for the whole page: the Near-Mage team rebuilt its copy around a single line after an earlier game's messaging showed too many things at once ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/kickstarting-a-game-in-2021-lessons-learned-from-near-mage-s-successful-campaign)). Failed pages in the set pitched several modes with equal weight; [Red Pine Lake](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/sakurastudios/red-pine-lake-asym-survival-horror/description) offered a story campaign, a horde mode and an asymmetric mode at once and raised £9,200 of £15,000.

**Few pages answer the two multiplayer worries, which makes an honest answer a cheap differentiator.** Fat Goblins is the only page that addresses server lifetime directly: "When no one's playing, the cost drops to almost nothing", plus a promise of locally hosted servers and dated public playtests ([Fat Goblins](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/kuju/fat-goblins-online-hat-based-pvp-inspired-by-fat-princess/description)). Springfield Hotel has no solo, matchmaking or server text at all. Reef Run should say plainly how online play is hosted, whether it works with fewer than four, whether a microphone is required, and where to find a group. Each of those statements has to be true of the build; the research did not verify them.

## Editor limits decide the visuals: 700 px images, WebP loops, linked badges

Kickstarter's story editor is a constrained rich-text editor. Confirmed features are headings with automatic anchors, bold, bulleted lists, images with a caption, URL and alt text, images used as section headers, and video by pasted URL only ([Text Editor 2.0](https://updates.kickstarter.com/text-editor-2-0/); [Kickstarter Help](https://help.kickstarter.com/en-us/articles/16236740-how-do-i-include-images-or-other-media-in-my-project-description-and-updates)). Tables, columns and styled buttons are not documented, so tier cards, the budget pie and the "exists today / money adds" comparison must be images. An image can carry a URL, which is how every campaign builds its Steam and Discord buttons.

**Images must be 50 MB or less and at least 700 px wide**, and Kickstarter recompresses them. Some animated GIFs display as static; Kickstarter's own fix is a WebP under 50 MB ([Kickstarter Help](https://help.kickstarter.com/en-us/articles/16236740-how-do-i-include-images-or-other-media-in-my-project-description-and-updates)). Gameplay loops should therefore be animated WebP. The project video accepts up to 5 GB and is shown from a 640×360 transcode, so HUD text is unreadable and captions must be large. Kickstarter's page guide says backers "tend to drop off after the first minute" and sets the title at 60 characters and the subtitle at 135 ([Kickstarter Updates](https://updates.kickstarter.com/level-up-your-campaign-in-2024/)). One real completion figure exists: 20% of 40,000 viewers finished The Wandering Village's video ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/the-wandering-village---kickstarter-success-without-a-playable-demo)). The pitch and the premise have to land in the first 20–30 seconds.

A 700 px graphic shown on a phone is scaled to about half size, so text baked into images needs to be roughly twice the size it should appear. This is arithmetic from the spec, not a sourced rule. Every image header should be followed by a line of real text.

**A new surface exists since August 2026.** Discovery Mode is a vertical video feed in Kickstarter's mobile apps; creators upload a separate 9:16 video in the Basics tab, with guidance of 60–90 seconds, captions, and key visuals kept away from the edges ([Kickstarter Creator Guide](https://start.kickstarter.com/hubfs/Mobile%20Discovery%20Mode%20-%20Creator%20Guide%20final.pdf)). The existing plan has no vertical cut. A sub-60-second vertical edit of the trailer satisfies the guide.

### The Steam button is standard practice, and Valve's rules shape it

The norm on funded pages is a pair of clickable Steam and Discord images placed once near the top, usually right after the first pitch paragraph, sometimes repeated at the bottom. Carnival Hunt, The Bold Club, Deadly Trick and Elevator put the buttons first; Fat Goblins, BLACKOUT and Combo Devils put them after the introduction; The Boo Croo and Springfield Hotel place them only late in the page. One or two Steam links per page is typical. Fat Goblins carries the only explicit line aimed at people who will not pledge: "Even if you don't plan on pledging, consider supporting the game by Wishlisting Fat Goblins on Steam and joining the Playtests!" ([Fat Goblins](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/kuju/fat-goblins-online-hat-based-pvp-inspired-by-fat-princess/description)). When a demo exists, the button says "play the demo" and becomes the first element ([Elevator](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/pixeltailgames/elevator-1/description)).

Reef Run should place the pair after the hook block and again at the end, use the Fat Goblins wording, and tag the Steam URL so the source is traceable. The button should not appear at all until the Steam page is live; a badge with no link reads as broken.

Valve's rules constrain both the button and the rewards. Steam keys may be promoted as crowdfunding rewards only for products with confirmed Steam distribution, and Steam logos must not be used for products not under a Steam Distribution Agreement. Campaigns promising keys must be **time-limited to a maximum of 30 days**, and keys are not for late-pledge or "slacker backer" periods. No more than 2,500 beta keys should be promised, and new developers must wait three weeks after creating an App ID before requesting keys ([Steamworks: Steam Keys](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/keys)). Valve's branding guidelines allow only approved logo artwork, standing alone, not combined with other words or graphics and not a prominent feature, with the trademark attribution line ([Steamworks: Branding](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/branding)). Campaigns routinely use art-styled buttons, and the researchers could not read the wording on any of them. The safe build for Reef Run's existing badge files is Valve's unmodified logo with clear space inside the button, and the attribution sentence in the story footer. Whether Valve supplies ready-made "Wishlist on Steam" badge art was not verified.

Valve's parity rule also matters for pricing: a Kickstarter price below the Steam price is fine if a comparable offer reaches Steam customers within a reasonable time ([Steamworks: Steam Keys](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/keys)). A launch discount covers that.

## The four-pack should carry the campaign, and only two stretch steps will be reached

The two closest comparables give opposite results on group tiers, and the difference is price per seat. Springfield Hotel's 2-, 4- and 8-packs were each cheaper per seat than the last, printed the per-seat price ("$13.75 ea"), added one cosmetic the single tier lacked, and drew **42% of backers**; the 2-pack outsold the 4-pack 187 to 107 ([Springfield Hotel rewards](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/msharkie/springfield-hotel-a-social-deduction-horror-game/rewards)). The Boo Croo's $80 four-pack cost $20 per copy against $15 for a single, and drew **6%** ([The Boo Croo rewards](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/synodic-arc/the-boo-croo-a-chaotic-4-player-party-game/rewards)). Reef Run's €49 pack works out to €12.25 per seat: more than the €12 early bird. For a game that needs four players, that is the tier to fix first.

The early bird needs a lower limit. The Boo Croo's $12 early bird, limited to 250, reached 182 in 30 days with 416 backers in total; it was still the most-backed tier at 44%. Springfield's limited $19 tier reached 380 of 385. A limit of 300 at Reef Run's scale will never close, which removes the scarcity and prices most backers at €12. Two cases are not a measured effect, but they point the same way.

Step-up and top tiers are weakly supported. Springfield's best seller was a $4 upgrade over the base game; The Boo Croo's $22 and $25 "game plus extras" tiers took 16% of backers together. Reef Run's €29 tier is nearly double the base and sits where a two-pack would. At the top, The Boo Croo's $400 design-a-cosmetic tier sold 2 of 15, and Springfield offered nothing above $99. The €5 no-game tier is harmless and nearly worthless: 7 of 416 backers at The Boo Croo.

Both campaigns show tiers as a graphic of stacked cards in the story, with a price badge, tier name, one line of content and "Limited to N", and repeat the detail in Kickstarter's reward cards. Both use the standard "Estimated delivery" month set six to seven months after the campaign ends and name the item "Steam Key". Springfield writes "+ All stretch goals" into every tier.

**The stretch ladder's shape is right; its reach is not.** The Boo Croo used 125 / 150 / 200 / 250 / 500 / 750% of a $10,000 goal, showed every goal from the start in one tall graphic, and finished at 156%, unlocking only the first two ([The Boo Croo](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/synodic-arc/the-boo-croo-a-chaotic-4-player-party-game)). Reef Run should put the two things backers most want, and that are cheapest to build, at 125% and 150%. The 400% step at €56,000 is harmless if it is an obviously large item and carries nothing backers assume is in the base game. No reward should depend on an unreached goal: The Boo Croo sold console copies before its console goal and needed an FAQ entry to explain what happens if it failed. Community goals that unlock one cosmetic each (Discord members, social mentions) are a low-cost pattern from the same page.

Kickstarter's co-founder warned in 2013 that stretch goals leave some projects "overwhelmed, over-budget, and behind schedule" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2013/08/13/kickstarter-warns-that-stretch-goals-muddy-the-waters.aspx)). The Boo Croo's risk text gives the wording that resolves it: "We've locked the scope to avoid feature creep... extras handled via stretch goals or free updates."

### Budget, risks and FAQ: short, specific, and framed as finishing

The small-team budget pattern is a six-slice pie by function with round percentages and one sentence. The Boo Croo shows Development 30%, Art 30%, Audio 10%, Marketing 10%, Rewards 10%, Contingency and Fees 10%. A €14,000 goal cannot be presented as the game's budget; both comparables frame it as finishing money on top of work already done. Springfield states "$135K USD spent in development so far" in its team story. Funded pages phrase the money as extending a playable game ("we will use the funds to expand on what we have", Elevator); failed ones phrased it as hiring the people who will build it (Red Pine Lake) or going full-time ([American Cooking Simulator](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/freedomburgergames/american-cooking-simulator-multiplayer/description)). The sample is small and the pattern is suggestive only. Fees and VAT belong in the pie as their own slice. Kickstarter takes 5% and payment processing "roughly 3-5%" ([Kickstarter Help](https://help.kickstarter.com/hc/en-us/articles/115005028634-What-are-the-fees)); the exact figures for a Dutch creator in EUR could not be re-confirmed.

Risks sections on both comparables run under 100 words: one framing sentence, three or four bolded risk names each with a mitigation, and a promise of monthly updates. Neither mentions player-count dependence, third-party voice and networking services, or what happens if a tiny team stops. Fat Goblins names the first: "The primary risk for Fat Goblins lies in its multiplayer focus." Reef Run can be more candid than its competitors here at no cost.

The Boo Croo's 15-question FAQ is a usable checklist: release date, platforms, demo, use of funds, exclusive content, delays, languages, online mode, when keys arrive, updates, feedback, Discord role timing, and the all-or-nothing charge rule ([Boo Croo FAQ](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/synodic-arc/the-boo-croo-a-chaotic-4-player-party-game/faqs)). Springfield funded with two entries. Neither answers "Can I play with fewer than four?", "Do I need a microphone?", "Can my friends join if only I backed?" or "Did you use AI?". Those are likely Reef Run's first questions, by inference rather than observed frequency.

## Disclose AI-assisted code up front; late discovery is what gets punished

Kickstarter asks two yes/no questions: "Does your campaign page contain any generative AI?" and "Will your final project contain any generative AI?" A yes to either requires a specific explanation, which is shown publicly under a set statement. The article does not define generative AI, does not mention code assistants, and says projects relying too heavily on generative AI may be declined or suspended. It was marked "Updated this week" on 16 November 2026 ([Kickstarter Help: AI policy](https://help.kickstarter.com/hc/en-us/articles/16848396410267)). Valve's content survey, rewritten in January 2026, covers AI content that ships and is consumed by players and says efficiency gains are "not the focus" ([Steamworks Content Survey](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/contentsurvey)).

Reef Run's code is AI-assisted, so the honest answer to the second question is yes, with the explanation limited to code. Relying on Steam's exemption is the reasoning House House gave for Big Walk. Its FAQ said chatbot output touched "well under 0.1%" of the code; Steam reviews went from 135 positive and 3 negative on 9 November 2026 to 426 positive and 366 negative a day later ([Dexerto](https://www.dexerto.com/gaming/big-walk-review-bombed-after-house-house-admits-ai-touched-well-under-0-1-of-code-3427480/)). Stackflow disclosed AI use three months after leaving Early Access and fell from **82% to 42% positive**; the reviewer's reading is that timing mattered more than the use ([Rogueliker](https://rogueliker.com/stackflow-late-ai-disclosure-reviews-backlash/)).

Player sentiment sets the expected cost. In a July 2026 survey of about 3,800 engaged Steam players, 31% reacted negatively to any AI disclosure and 8% would not buy; coding help was the most-named acceptable use, and **56% said AI coding helpers should be disclosed** ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/what-do-steam-fans-really-think-about)). The sample is self-selected. An up-front code-only disclosure loses some backers and removes the larger risk that someone "discovers" it later.

A "No AI" line and the No Gen AI seal are both off the table. Springfield Hotel, the nearest rival, defines the claim to include code: "We do not use AI tools to generate art, design, or code" ([Springfield Hotel](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/msharkie/springfield-hotel-a-social-deduction-horror-game)). A narrower true claim is available: no generative AI art, audio, voices or writing in the game. Before making it, each licensed asset pack's listing needs checking for an AI declaration; that check has not been done.

The first question needs its own honest answer. If the campaign copy or graphics were produced with an AI tool, the page answer is also yes. Rewriting the copy in the team's own words so the answer is truthfully no is the stronger position beside a rival selling "No AI". Stackflow's three-sentence structure, delivered before launch, is the model: what used AI, that a human reviewed it, and what did not use AI. A short form for the Kickstarter field: "Game code was written with the help of an AI coding assistant and reviewed and tested by us. No generative AI is used for art, audio, voices, music or writing. Art and audio are licensed asset packs and our own in-engine renders." The same statement should appear in the story, the FAQ, and later on Steam and Discord. No live Kickstarter game page disclosing a coding assistant was found, so this wording is drafted, not copied from precedent.

## What the evidence does not support

**A good page does not fund a campaign.** American Cooking Simulator had a demo button first, nine GIFs, a clear loop section and a public counter showing 3,441 wishlists; it raised €6,382 of €20,000. Redhowl, with a demo and proximity voice chat, raised €645 of €4,000 ([American Cooking Simulator](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/freedomburgergames/american-cooking-simulator-multiplayer/description); [Redhowl](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/benomstudio/redhowl/description)). Deadly Trick had the largest page in the set and barely funded. The live test closest to Reef Run is [Mischief House](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/ajamiz/mischief-house-do-chores-blame-your-friends/description): an online party game with a social-deduction mode, a solo developer, a €14,384 goal, a claimed 1,000-plus wishlists and 700 Discord members, and **€8 from 2 backers with 19 days left** on 16 November 2026. Its page is thin, so page and audience effects cannot be separated, but wishlists in the low thousands do not predict a €14,000 raise. Its result, due around 5 December, is worth checking before launch. The 2025 market review calls community building "the cornerstone of crowdfunding" and cites a demo as "a big boon" ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/what-worked-for-video-game-crowdfunding)).

**No measured evidence exists on page layout.** Nothing found tests section order, GIF count, word count, image versus text headers, captions or work-in-progress labels against conversion. Most layout advice in circulation comes from vendors with no published method. The page order above describes what funded pages did; failed pages used the same formats competently.

**Nobody has measured whether a wishlist button reduces pledges.** Opinion is split between "it gives people an easier way out" and "it catches people who would not pledge". Placement near the top is common practice, not a proven optimum, and a public wishlist counter did not save American Cooking Simulator.

**Popular thresholds have no traceable source.** "30% in 48 hours means 85% success" and "76% of successful campaigns used early birds" are repeated by blogs with no dataset. No verified figure exists for the mobile share of Kickstarter traffic. Success-rate figures conflict across sources; the one with a named dataset puts the long-run video-game rate at 32%, with December launches at 28% and February at 35% ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/kickstarter-in-2025-and-before)).

**Hidden stretch goals and face-to-camera video are unsupported either way.** Both comparables showed every goal up front; no small 2025–2026 campaign with locked reveals was confirmed. No data compares game campaigns with and without a founder on camera. No controlled study links stretch goals to late delivery.

**Springfield Hotel's average pledge is not a benchmark.** Its visible tiers sum to about $22,000 against an $84,908 total, and the gap is unexplained. The Boo Croo's $38 average was lifted by plush toys. A digital-only campaign should plan on a lower average unless packs sell.

**Several rules are unverified.** Kickstarter's Rules, Prohibited Items and fees pages returned errors to automated reading, so nothing here quotes them. The prototype and no-renderings rule is confirmed only for hardware and product design ([Kickstarter Help](https://help.kickstarter.com/en-us/articles/16236428-what-are-the-rules-for-hardware-and-product-design-projects)), not games. The ban on contests, raffles and coupons rests on secondhand quotes. No official rule on stretch goals, on "percent off retail" claims, or on external store links was found. Reward-image specs, the project image's exact minimum, FAQ limits, pre-launch page mechanics and whether Kickstarter counts coding assistants as generative AI are all open. How Kickstarter's VAT handling treats digital-only rewards from a Dutch individual is not stated; the April 2026 change lets creators price VAT-inclusive and handle VAT themselves ([Kickstarter release notes](https://start.kickstarter.com/kickstarter-release-notes/vat-flexibility-in-pledge-manager)), and a Dutch tax adviser should confirm the rest.

## Recommended changes to the existing plan

| Plan item | Change | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| €49 four-pack | Reprice so the per-seat price is below the €12 early bird (about €44, or €11 a seat), print "€11 each" on the card, add one small pack-only cosmetic, and make it the featured tier | Springfield packs 42% of backers; Boo Croo pack 6% |
| €29 tier | Replace with a two-pack at a per-seat price below €15, or cut it to a small step-up of a few euros over the base game | Springfield 2-pack outsold its 4-pack; its best seller was +$4 |
| €12 early bird, limit 300 | Lower the limit to roughly 100–150 | Boo Croo sold 182 of 250 with 416 backers |
| €199, limit 25 | Lower the limit to about 10; plan revenue on 2–5 sold | Boo Croo $400 tier sold 2 of 15 |
| €5, €15, €99 | Keep; expect almost nothing from €5 | Boo Croo 7 of 416 at $5 |
| Stretch ladder 125/150/200/250/400% | Keep the steps; show all goals from the start; put the cheapest, most wanted items at 125% and 150%; state that goals ship as free updates; add two or three one-cosmetic community goals | Boo Croo ended at 156% of a near-identical ladder |
| Duration and Late Pledges | 30 days or fewer; Late Pledges off, or excluding key rewards; no more than 2,500 beta keys promised | Valve key rules |
| Steam badge | Show only once the Steam page is live and the game is under the Distribution Agreement; unmodified Valve logo; attribution line in the footer; place after the hook and at the end | Valve branding rules; observed placement |
| AI disclosure | Answer yes for the final project, code only; no "No AI" wording or seal; team rewrites page copy; same statement in story, AI field and FAQ | Big Walk, Stackflow, GameDiscoverCo survey |
| Media | Animated WebP for loops; images at 700 px or wider with doubled text size; add a sub-60-second 9:16 Discovery Mode video | Kickstarter Help; Discovery Mode guide |
| Launch month | Avoid a December start | 28% success in December against 35% in February |
| Before submitting | Read Kickstarter's Rules, Prohibited Items and fees pages in a browser; check asset packs for AI declarations; confirm VAT treatment | Pages unreadable to the researchers |

The repricing figures are judgements from two comparable campaigns, not measured optima.

## Conclusion

The research moves the weight of the decision away from the page. Reef Run's plan already matches what funded pages look like, and the remaining page work is specific and small: fix the pack price, tighten two limits, cut a vertical video, build the buttons to Valve's rules, and write the AI statement before anyone asks. None of it changes the odds as much as the audience does, and the one live campaign in Reef Run's exact position shows what a €14,000 goal looks like without one.

Two things are new since the October teardown. First, the AI question has shifted from "should we disclose" to "when": November 2026 produced a review collapse over ten lines of assisted code, and the lesson from both cases is that the disclosure a team controls is cheaper than the one it does not. Second, Reef Run's most ordinary facts have become differentiators. Almost no competitor says how its online play is hosted, whether a short-handed group can play, or what happens if the team stops. A page that answers those plainly, beside real footage of a playable game, is offering the thing backers screen for after Alzara: evidence that the game exists and will arrive.
