# Successful Video Game Kickstarter Budgets and Use of Funds

**Research checked:** 2026-10-18  
**Purpose:** Find what successful games with comparable mechanics raised, what their creators said the money would fund, and what they later disclosed about actual costs. This supplements [`../01_MARKET_RESEARCH.md`](../01_MARKET_RESEARCH.md), which already covers campaign outcomes and market comparables.

## How to read these figures

- **Goal** is the campaign's all-or-nothing Kickstarter target. It is not necessarily the total development budget.
- **Raised** is the gross Kickstarter total shown by Kickstarter. It is not the amount available to spend after fees, taxes, failed payments, reward costs, or other obligations.
- **Planned use** is what the creator said before or during the campaign. It is not an audited account of final spending.
- **Actual use** is included only where the studio later published figures. The disclosures below are creator-reported, not independently audited.
- Campaign totals and averages are left in their original currencies. Average pledge is calculated as raised divided by Kickstarter backers; it can be skewed by reward mix and add-ons.
- The cases are selected examples, not a representative sample. Do not use their success or overfunding multiples as a forecast for REEF RUN.

Kicktraq, the tool recommended on page 3 of the supplied guide, is useful for campaign histories and daily pledge patterns, but its captured totals can differ from Kickstarter's final result. For *Day of Dragons*, Kicktraq shows $533,890 and 12,121 backers while Kickstarter shows $533,938 and 12,123. Use Kickstarter's final result as canonical and Kicktraq for the timing/trend view.

## What creators said about budgets and spending

### The Flame in the Flood: a planned allocation, not actuals

For the $150,000 goal, the creator's initial forecast was:

| Planned category | Share | Approx. amount at the $150,000 goal |
|---|---:|---:|
| Taxes and fees | 32% | $48,000 |
| Development (art, programming, design) | 24% | $36,000 |
| Office, software, administration | 20% | $30,000 |
| Reward fulfillment | 11% | $16,500 |
| Music | 9% | $13,500 |
| Shows and conventions | 4% | $6,000 |

These amounts are arithmetic applied to the creator's approximate percentages, not audited costs. The creator said their spreadsheet was updated as pledges came in, physical reward mix could move fulfillment costs, and fixed administrative costs would become a smaller share if funding exceeded the goal. Therefore, applying those same percentages to the final $251,647 would be misleading.

### LOST EMBER: the clearest actual budget disclosure found

Mooneye Studios later reported:

- About **€800,000** in total development costs, excluding patches and post-release marketing.
- About **€220,000** from Kickstarter **after taxes and reward production**. That is roughly 27.5% of the reported development budget; it was not enough on its own to pay for the full game.
- Other reported sources: €130,000 public funding, €130,000 private investor, €130,000 bank loan, €70,000 contract/side work, €70,000 private loans, €25,000 personal funds, and €25,000 awards. The studio said about **€450,000** of the total budget had to be paid back in some way after release.
- About **€105,000** of pre/during-release marketing: €40,000 Facebook ads, €30,000 conventions, €13,000 YouTube ads, €10,000 PR, €5,000 Instagram ads, €5,000 banner ads, and €2,000 Twitter ads. This figure excludes post-release marketing and patch costs, as does the €800,000 development budget.
- Staffing, delay, and porting needs grew beyond the original estimates, so the studio continued seeking additional financing after Kickstarter.

This is a useful example of a campaign headline concealing a much larger production plan. It is not a direct genre or scale benchmark for REEF RUN, and the marketing figure should not be copied as a recommended budget.

### Comparable asymmetric games: public totals, little public accounting

- **Friday the 13th** is the closest licensed comparison. A developer interview says the team had money for *Summer Camp* but needed more to adapt it into the licensed game. Its $823,704 Kickstarter result does not disclose the game's total budget or actual expenditure by category.
- **Carnival Hunt** is the closest original asymmetric Kickstarter example in this set. Its 1,470 backers pledged $122,426 against a $50,000 goal. Stretch goals expanded obligations, but the public pages reviewed do not provide actual costs for development, rewards, fees, or the console port.
- **Day of Dragons** shows how an unexpectedly large result can change scope. The creator says the initial goal was for three dragon models, but the amount raised funded a much larger rebuild. A developer update describes the categories of work, but not their dollar amounts.
- **Moon Hunters** is useful for a small co-op team. Its creator explicitly tied the $45,000 goal to campaign labor and roughly two more development months, while relying on other sources for the wider production. That is a more realistic way to interpret a modest Kickstarter goal than treating it as a full game's total cost.

## Implications for REEF RUN's funding model

1. Do not infer full game cost from an ask or campaign total. Across these cases, the goal ranged from a slice of production to work on a pre-existing game, and only one studio in this review later disclosed a full-project budget.
2. Do not treat gross pledges as usable cash. Track platform/payment deductions, tax treatment, failed payments, rewards, and fulfillment separately. The full *LOST EMBER* postmortem is unusually helpful because it reports a post-tax/reward Kickstarter contribution and other funding sources.
3. Keep the base-funded promise independently deliverable. *Day of Dragons* is an extreme example of a windfall changing the production plan; its 4,450% result should not shape REEF RUN's baseline scope.
4. Show a transparent planned budget even if competitors did not. Mark it as a forecast, price founder labor, disclose outside funding or unpaid work, and later report actuals against plan if the campaign is funded.
5. For REEF RUN's closest peers, observed campaign goals/raises can inform backer counts and pledge mixes, but **they do not provide reliable per-feature cost estimates**. Use contractor quotes, labor hours, and the build's remaining work to set the actual funding goal.

## Source reliability and limitations

- **High confidence:** Kickstarter's own final goals, gross pledges, backer counts, and campaign dates.
- **Medium-high confidence:** creator statements in direct interviews and studio postmortems. These are first-party disclosures but are not audited accounts.
- **Medium confidence:** Kicktraq campaign history. It is a third-party snapshot and may differ slightly from Kickstarter's final figures; its own pages say trend estimates are not projections.
- **Unknown:** itemized actual spending for *Friday the 13th*, *Carnival Hunt*, and *Day of Dragons* in the public sources reviewed. Do not fill these gaps by applying assumed percentages.
